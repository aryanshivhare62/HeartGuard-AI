## 2. In-Depth Study Syntheses

### 2.1 Cross-Dataset Generalization & Benchmark Provenance
- **Detrano et al. (1989)** established the multicenter heart disease database across Cleveland, Budapest, Zurich, and Long Beach. Their historical findings confirmed that diagnostic discrimination varied widely across geographic sites due to differences in referral patterns and clinical disease prevalence.
- **Wilson et al. (1998)** developed the Framingham 10-year risk prediction model based on long-term follow-up of community volunteers. It addresses prospective incident disease rather than catheter-confirmed anatomical stenosis. Conflating Detrano's diagnostic target with Wilson's prognostic endpoint in a single model is scientifically invalid.
- **Siontis et al. (2015)** proved that external validation of clinical prediction models is overwhelmingly rare (only 17% independently validated) and systematically results in performance deterioration (median AUC loss of 0.05). This confirms the core premise of HeartGuard AI's research gap: random holdout validation severely inflates expected clinical performance.

### 2.2 Algorithm Selection & Complexity Realism
- **Christodoulou et al. (2019)** reviewed 71 clinical studies comparing 282 ML models to logistic regression and demonstrated that when validation protocols are rigorous and free of data leakage, ML models rarely exhibit superior discrimination over penalized logistic regression on modest sample sizes.
- **Weng et al. (2017)** and **Alaa et al. (2019)** demonstrated that when sample sizes are expansive ($N > 300,000$), nonlinear gradient boosted trees and automated ensembles capture meaningful multi-factor interactions that modestly improve discrimination over traditional risk scores.
- **Rudin (2019)** demonstrated that for structured tabular problems, complex black-box models rarely outperform well-tuned interpretable models, and relying on post-hoc explanations can create a false sense of security.

### 2.3 Calibration & Reliability
- **Van Calster et al. (2016, 2019)** established that high discrimination (AUC) does not prevent severe miscalibration. In clinical practice, decision thresholds depend on predicted absolute probabilities. HeartGuard AI adopts their four-level hierarchy, evaluating calibration slope, intercept, Brier score, and loess curves.
- **Ovadia et al. (2019)** demonstrated that post-hoc calibration methods fit under the assumption of identical distributions fail when applied under dataset shift. HeartGuard AI specifically evaluates calibration degradation during cross-dataset transfer.

### 2.4 Explainability, Fairness, and Input Verification
- **Lundberg et al. (2017, 2018)** established SHAP as the mathematically sound standard for additive feature attributions, with TreeSHAP providing efficient computation for gradient-boosted ensembles.
- **Rajkomar et al. (2018)** and **Woodward (2019)** documented that clinical prediction models trained on male-predominant populations often exhibit disparate sensitivity and higher false-negative rates in female cohorts. HeartGuard AI incorporates disaggregated subgroup auditing by sex and age.
- **Lee et al. (2018)** showed that multivariate Mahalanobis distance effectively identifies out-of-distribution inputs without requiring retraining, supplying the basis for HeartGuard AI's input validation guardrails.
